WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

You are going to build a model that answers questions like:

    STATE:      "I was charged twice for order 4411. Please refund one charge."
    QUESTION:   Which team should handle this?
    OPTIONS:    1. billing  2. shipping  3. technical support
    ANSWER:     billing, with probabilities {billing: 0.91, shipping: 0.06, ...}

...across text, images, audio and video, in a single forward pass, on a laptop.

The model you are copying is `akhilaaa3/Jev-Omni`: a 12-billion-parameter
decision classifier built on Google's Gemma 4 12B. It is genuinely good. It is
also expensive -- roughly 24 GB of weights in bf16, a CUDA GPU required, and a
reported 83 ms per text decision on an H200, which is a datacenter accelerator
that costs about $2-3/hour to rent.

You want the same *interface* on something like 1/34th of the parameters, that
runs on a Raspberry Pi.

That is what this guide is for.

================================================================================
PART 0 -- THE THIRTY-SECOND VERSION
================================================================================

If you read nothing else, read this.

WHAT JEV-OMNI IS
    A language model with its text-generation ability replaced by a classifier
    head. You give it a situation and a question with options; it gives back a
    probability for every option. It never writes a sentence. That is the entire
    trick, and it is worth ~99% of the cost saving.

WHAT WE ARE CHANGING
    Backbone:   Gemma 4 12B  ->  Liquid AI LFM2.5-350M   (12B -> 350M, ~34x)
    Head:       Linear(3840, 256)  ->  Linear(1024, 256)
    Training:   2.1 billion trainable LoRA params, 4 GPUs  ->  ~6 million, 1 GPU
    Latency:    83 ms on H200  ->  tens of ms on a laptop CPU
    Cost:       needs a datacenter GPU  ->  runs on a Raspberry Pi 5

WHAT WE ARE NOT CHANGING
    The idea. The head is still Linear(hidden, 256). The prompt still numbers the
    options. The output is still a softmax over 256 slots. Everything the original
    author got right, we keep.

THE HEAD IS ALMOST FREE
    LFM2.5-350M has 354,483,968 parameters. Our decision head adds 262,400.
    That is 0.074% of the network. You could attach this head to a 10B model and
    it would still be invisible in the parameter count. This is why "make it
    smaller" is not a desperate optimisation -- it is mostly about which
    pretrained backbone you start from.

THE ONE THING THAT MATTERS MOST
    A decision model is not a chatbot that happens to be small. It is a
    classifier with a language model's knowledge. Everything else -- the prompt,
    the options, the temperature -- is detail compared to the base checkpoint you
    fine-tune and the calibration you fit afterwards.

================================================================================
PART 1 -- VOCABULARY, BECAUSE THE TERMS WILL OTHERWISE STOP YOU
================================================================================

1.1 TOKEN
-------
A token is a chunk of text. Not a word -- a *chunk*. The most common words are
one token. Rare words get split into pieces. "Unbelievably" might be 3 tokens.

Why it matters: models read and write tokens, not letters. Every cost formula
you will meet is denominated in tokens. A 2,000-token document is about 1,500
words of English prose.

LFM2.5-350M has a vocabulary of 65,536 tokens. That means every piece of text
becomes a list of integers in the range 0-65535.

1.2 TOKENIZER
------------
The component that turns text into tokens and back. It is a fixed, learned
lookup table plus some rules about splitting. It is NEVER trained. It is part of
the model checkpoint.

    tokenizer.encode("hello world")  ->  [15339, 1917]     (2 tokens)
    tokenizer.decode([15339, 1917]) ->  "hello world"

Pin your tokenizer with your model. If you upgrade the tokenizer, every number
you have measured is now wrong.

1.3 EMBEDDING / HIDDEN STATE / HIDDEN SIZE
-----------------------------------------
Neural networks turn tokens into vectors of numbers. A vector of 1,024 numbers
is a "hidden state" of width 1,024.

`hidden_size` is the width of that vector at every layer.

    LFM2.5-350M    hidden_size = 1024
    LFM2.5-2.6B    hidden_size = 2048
    Gemma 4 12B    hidden_size = 3840

This number is the single most important one for our purposes, because the size
of the decision head is exactly `hidden_size * 256 + 256`.

1.4 LAYER
---------
One transformation of the vector. A model stacks many of them. LFM2.5-350M has
16. Gemma 4 12B has 48.

Not all layers compute the same thing. LFM2.5-350M's 16 layers are:

    conv, conv, full_attention, conv, conv, full_attention, conv, conv,
    full_attention, conv, full_attention, conv, full_attention, conv,
    full_attention, conv
             ^^^^^^^^^^^^^  6 attention layers
    ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^  10 short-convolution layers

That is the "hybrid" architecture. The conv layers are cheap local pattern
detectors. The attention layers let distant parts of the input talk to each
other. Liquid AI found this mix by running an architecture search against real
edge hardware latency, rather than by guessing.

1.5 FORWARD PASS
---------------
One run of the input through all the layers. This is what "inference" means.

A language model runs a forward pass once per generated token -- a 100-token
answer is 100 forward passes. A decision model runs the forward pass **once**,
and that one pass produces the whole answer. This is where the speed comes from.

1.6 LOGIT
---------
A raw, unnormalised score. Higher means "more likely".

    logits = [2.1, -0.3, 0.7]        # not probabilities, do not sum to 1

1.7 SOFTMAX
-----------
Turns logits into probabilities that sum to 1.

    p_i = exp(z_i) / sum_j exp(z_j)

Given logits [2.1, -0.3, 0.7] you get roughly [0.76, 0.07, 0.19].

1.8 LOGIT / LOG-LIKELIHOOD
-------------------------
The log of a probability, before it is turned back into a probability. A
language model's native output is logits over its whole vocabulary -- 65,536
numbers for LFM2.5-350M. When someone says "the model scored option A", they
usually mean "the log-likelihood of the tokens of option A was highest".

9.8 LOGPROB
-----------
The log-probability the model assigned to a token it actually produced. This is
what you get from an LLM API. It is a *generated* number: the model chose to
write those digits. It is not a statement about how confident the model was.

1.10 THE CENTRAL TRAP
--------------------
This is the single most important idea in the whole guide, and it is the reason
decision models exist at all.

    An LLM can WRITE the text "91%".
    That does not mean there is a 91% chance it is right.

The model's log-probability of emitting the characters "9", "1", "%" says how
surprising that *string* was. It is a statement about text, not about truth. A
model that is systematically overconfident will cheerfully write "95% confident"
and be wrong half the time, and the probability it assigns to its own digits will
agree with that claim.

A decision model fixes this by never generating the confidence at all. It reads
the number out of an internal representation and softmaxes it. You cannot
generate a probability you did not compute, and there is no "as an AI language
model" refusal path, because there is no generation.

1.11 CALIBRATION
----------------
A model is *calibrated* when, when it says 80% confident, it is right about 80%
of the time.

Two models can both be 85% accurate. Model A is confident when right and unsure
when wrong. Model B is 95% confident every single time. Same accuracy, wildly
different usefulness -- only A can be used to route work, escalate cases, or set
a threshold.

Calibration is not accuracy. You need both, and you must measure them separately.

1.12 ECE (EXPECTED CALIBRATION ERROR)
------------------------------------
The standard summary number. Split predictions into buckets by confidence; in
each bucket compare confidence to actual accuracy; average the gaps, weighted by
bucket size.

    ECE = sum over buckets of (share of predictions in bucket) * |confidence - accuracy|

Lower is better. Zero is perfect.

CAVEAT, and it is a real one: ECE depends on how many buckets you use and where
you put the edges. DecisionBench reports ECE over 10 equal-width bins in its
table and draws its chart with 5. Those two numbers are NOT comparable. Always
check the binning before comparing two ECE figures.

1.13 BRIER SCORE
----------------
Mean squared error between your predicted distribution and the true one-hot
answer, normalised by the number of options. Lower is better.

Some people argue Brier is better than ECE for this application, and the
`typed-decisions` benchmark makes a strong case: a model that always predicts the
base rate is *perfectly calibrated by construction* (it is never surprised) yet
completely useless. Reporting ECE alone lets that model look good. Report both.

1.14 KL DIVERGENCE
-----------------
How far your distribution is from a reference distribution. `typed-decisions`
reports it as the primary distribution-quality metric, and it is the metric that
moved most when they switched a classifier to soft targets: KL fell by a third.

1.15 LoRA (LOW-RANK ADAPTATION)
-------------------------------
Instead of retraining all 354M parameters, you add a small trainable "sidecar" of
maybe 6M parameters and freeze the rest.

The intuition: the update to a big weight matrix usually has low rank -- you can
express it as a product of two small matrices. So you train only those.

    W' = W + (alpha/r) * B * A

where W is frozen (354M params), A is r x hidden and B is hidden x r are trained.

r=16 on a 1024-wide model gives ~6M trainable parameters -- 1.7% of the model.

Why this matters here: you can train the whole thing on one consumer GPU in
minutes, and you can throw away the adapter and keep the original model.

1.16 QLoRA
----------
LoRA where the frozen base is also quantised to 4 bits during training. Uses
roughly the same memory as training the adapter alone. Matters when your GPU is
small. Not needed for a 350M model on a 24 GB card, but useful on a 12 GB one.

1.17 bf16 / fp32 / int8 / int4
-----------------------------
How many bits per number.

    fp32   32 bits   full precision, baseline
    bf16   16 bits   what these models ship in. ~3 significant decimal digits.
                     Range like fp32, so no overflow. The default.
    int8    8 bits   half the memory, tiny quality loss
    int4    4 bits   quarter the memory, small quality loss

bf16 has a subtlety that will bite you: it can represent 1.0 and 1.0078 but
nothing between. When you compute probabilities in bf16 you get ~3 decimal
digits. That is why our head is fp32 even when the backbone is bf16.

1.18 TEMPERATURE
----------------
A single number you divide logits by before the softmax.

    p = softmax(z / T)

T < 1 sharpens (more confident). T > 1 softens (less confident).

Crucially, temperature CANNOT change which option wins -- softmax is monotonic,
so the argmax is invariant. Temperature only reshapes the distribution around
the winner. That makes it a safe knob to fit after training without changing any
answers.

1.19 CONVOLUTION vs ATTENTION
-----------------------------
Convolution looks at a fixed small window of neighbouring positions. Cheap,
local, fixed receptive field.

Attention lets every position look at every other position, weighted by learned
relevance. Expensive, global.

LFM2.5 uses mostly convolution with some attention. That is why it is fast on
CPUs. Gemma 4 12B uses a 5:1 mix of sliding-window and full attention.

1.20 KV CACHE
-------------
When a transformer processes a prompt, each layer stores the "key" and "value"
vectors it computed, so it does not recompute them. This is the cache.

LFM2.5 also has a *convolution* cache: the last 3 positions' worth of convolution
state (`conv_L_cache: 3`). If you branch a batch -- process several candidate
answers in parallel -- you must copy BOTH caches. Copying only the attention KV
and forgetting the conv state produces a model that is subtly, silently wrong.
The `rlcd` project documented exactly this bug and its fix.

1.21 JSON SCHEMA
----------------
A machine-readable description of a data shape. We use it to describe the
options a question may be answered with.

    {
      "type": "object",
      "properties": {
        "team": {
          "type": "string",
          "description": "Which team should handle this?",
          "enum": ["billing", "shipping", "technical"]
        },
        "urgent": {
          "type": "boolean",
          "description": "True only if immediate action is needed"
        }
      },
      "required": ["team", "urgent"],
      "additionalProperties": false
    }

1.22 TYPED DECISIONS: noul, choice, score
------------------------------------------
The three question types this whole ecosystem converged on. They trace back to
TypeSafe AI's "System One" primitives.

    noul    -- a yes/no statement. Answer: P(true), one number.
    choice  -- one of N named options. Answer: a distribution over options.
    score   -- an ordered rubric ("calm" < "frustrated" < "angry").
               Answer: a distribution over levels PLUS the expected level, which
               can fall between two levels. Expected 1.4 means "between
               Frustrated and Very angry".

Treating these three types explicitly, rather than as free text, is most of what
makes these systems usable: your code gets a guaranteed type.

2.2 THE HEAD -- VERBATIM
------------------------
From `jev_omni.py`, unedited:

    class _Head256(torch.nn.Module):
        """Decision head: normalise the last hidden state, one logit per option slot."""
        def __init__(self, hidden):
            super().__init__()
            self.register_buffer("mu", torch.zeros(1, hidden))
            self.register_buffer("sd", torch.ones(1, hidden))
            self.linear = torch.nn.Linear(hidden, 256, dtype=torch.float32)

        def forward(self, features, counts):
            z = self.linear((features.float() - self.mu) / self.sd)
            return z.masked_fill(torch.arange(256, device=z.device)[None] >= counts[:, None], -1e30)

That is the whole model-specific part. Four things are happening:

(a) TAKE THE LAST HIDDEN STATE.
    A forward hook on the text backbone captures
    `out.last_hidden_state[:, -1].float()`. The final position, because that is
    where the model would have predicted the answer.

(b) STANDARDISE IT. `(features - mu) / sd`, with mu and sd stored as *buffers*,
    not parameters -- fixed statistics, never trained. This keeps the head's
    input well-scaled regardless of what the backbone is doing.

(c) PROJECT TO 256 SLOTS. `Linear(3840, 256)`. These are NOT vocabulary tokens.
    They are 256 *positional option slots*: slot 0 means "option 1", slot 1 means
    "option 2", and so on.

(d) MASK AND SOFTMAX. Slots at or beyond the real option count get -1e30, i.e.
    probability 0. The real slots get softmaxed.

Parameter count: 3840*256 + 256 = 983,456. Against an 11,959,730,224-parameter
model, that is 0.008%.

2.3 WHY SLOT LOGITS, NOT TOKEN LOGITS
-------------------------------------
This is the design decision that makes one checkpoint generalise.

If the head scored the *text* of each option, then "billing" and "shipping" would
need to be known vocabulary, and an unseen label would be unscoreable.

Because it scores *positions in a list*, the same 256 weights handle any
question with any 2-256 options. A model fine-tuned on support tickets can answer
a question about satellite imagery it has never seen, because the head does not
care what the words mean.

The whole method rests on the prompt putting the options in a known order:

    {state}

    ---

    QUESTION: {question}

    OPTIONS:
    1. billing
    2. shipping
    3. technical support

    Reply with only the number of the correct option (1-3).
    Output a single number and nothing else.

The prompt's numbering and the head's slot indexing are two halves of one
contract. Change the renderer and you must retrain.

2.6 WHAT IT IS NOT
------------------
Jev-Omni is *not* affiliated with TypeSafe AI. Its own card says so, and says
nothing was trained on Jev output.

TypeSafe AI's "Jev" is a closed, hosted product at $0.042 per million input
tokens. It has no published weights, no published training method, and no paper.
An independent reverse-engineering effort (`archerhume.com`, 17 Sep 2026, based
on 10,000 API calls) concluded the design is "a causal transformer repurposed for
decisions: shared-state encoding, isolated question branches, and direct
probability readouts". The parts it could confirm -- parallel probabilities,
state reuse across questions, question isolation, option interaction before
choosing -- are exactly the parts Jev-Omni implements. The backbone question it
could not answer.

So: Jev-Omni is an independent reimplementation of a *design pattern*, and the
pattern is what you can legitimately copy.

3.1 WHAT LFM2 IS
---------------
From the LFM2 technical report (arXiv 2511.23404, 28 Nov 2025):

    "Using hardware-in-the-loop architecture search under edge latency and memory
     constraints, we obtain a compact hybrid backbone that combines gated short
     convolutions with a small number of grouped query attention blocks, delivering
     up to 2x faster prefill and decode on CPUs compared to similarly sized
     models."

Three things in that sentence matter:

- *Hardware-in-the-loop search*. The architecture was chosen by measuring latency
  and memory on real devices, not by a proxy loss.
- *Gated short convolutions*. Most layers are cheap local filters.
- *Grouped-query attention*. A small number of expensive global layers.

PART 4 -- THE EXISTING PRIOR ART, AND WHAT IT TAUGHT US
================================================================================

Before designing anything, we read what other people built. There are dozens.
The most instructive:

4.1 notnotsamuel/LFM2.5-350M-RLCD   <- the closest thing to our goal
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is almost certainly what you meant by "RCLDs".

Inference-only. The base weights are byte-identical to LiquidAI/LFM2.5-350M at
revision 9e6c6ccf47cd318696e137d381a7ded8fe4df09f. No training at all.

Its method: prefill the context once, deep-copy the hybrid cache (attention KV
*and* the short-convolution state), branch the cache, score every JSON-Schema
candidate value in one batch, sum the log-softmax over each candidate's full
token sequence, take the argmax per field, assemble the JSON in Python.

Its own measured results, 28 boolean fields, FP16:

    M2 Max     393.20 ms constrained vs 3326.95 ms AR   =  8.46x
    L40S        54.12 ms vs 3404.48 ms                   = 62.91x
    H100        43.76 ms vs 2586.97 ms                   = 59.12x
    field accuracy 60.7% / 64.3% / 60.7% vs 53.6% autoregressive

And the honest caveats, which we quote because they matter more than the speedup:

  - "Valid JSON does not mean correct answers: neither method produced a fully
     correct 28-field object."
  - On its 12-case diagnostic suite the constrained method was *faster* but
     *less accurate*: 77.8% vs 80.6% field accuracy.
  - With 255 candidate options on a Mac, constrained inference was 3.23x SLOWER.
  - "They are not calibrated confidence. No calibration evaluation was performed
     and no normalized probability API is exposed."
  - It explicitly does NOT reproduce TypeSafe's training method.

THE KEY LESSON: constraining the decoder gives you *structure* for free, but it
gives you neither accuracy nor calibration. And it does nothing to shrink the
model. We want the head approach AND a smaller backbone, not this.

4.2 akhilaaa3/Jev-Omni
----------------------
The head design we are copying. 12B. 87.57% on DecisionBench Medium, ECE 0.0400.

4.3 jaredpalmer/kev   (0.8B / 4B / 9B / 27B, Apache-2.0)
---------------------------------------------------------
The most carefully documented open family. Key design: a **pointer head** that
scores each option's own `</opt>` hidden state against the question's `<decide>`
hidden state, rather than scoring fixed slots. Options are labelled with single
tokens A, B, C...

Their recipe: LoRA r=16 alpha=32, two epochs, lr 1e-4, ~20 minutes on one H100
for the 0.8B. They ship a fitted temperature per checkpoint.

Their honesty about scale, which we quote directly:

    "It is still a sub-1B model. On the development splits it trails Jev
     everywhere it can be compared: documents 0.842 vs 0.868, hard-v1 0.594 vs
     0.777, devtools-v1 0.602 vs 0.713, out of domain 0.648 vs 0.857."

and:

    "Out of domain it is a sub-1B model. Knowledge (MMLU 0.41) and paraphrase
     (PAWS 0.59) are near the untrained base."

and:

    "Out of domain it is still a sub-1B model: use Kev-4B for accuracy; use this
     one where memory rules the 4B out, and measure on your own data."

and the single most useful sentence in this whole guide:

    "Probabilities are usable in-domain; treat them as advisory elsewhere."

Their 0.8B card also documents fifteen registered training rounds before the
shipped one, and that stacking a second delta on top of a first one *eroded* the
first ("the second delta eroded the first: seed 1's documents lower bound was
-2.03 pp against a -2 pp floor"). Stacking LoRA deltas is not free.

4.4 frontier-infra/jebadiah (4B / 9B, Apache-2.0)
--------------------------------------------------
Their approach differs: option labels are single tokens, and the answer is the
distribution over those label tokens at the last prompt position, read in fp32
from the last hidden state, then temperature-scaled per type.

They ship three temperatures: choice 1.12, noul 1.33, score 1.20.

The finding we care most about is about *how* to fit that temperature:

    "The `hard` fit minimises NLL against the argmax label and sharpens
     (T 0.68 / 0.96 / 0.83). The `train` fit minimises NLL against the training
     target, the soft or ordinal distribution the model was taught, and softens
     (T 1.12 / 1.33 / 1.20). v2 applies `train` ... because the ordinal target is
     what makes rubric probabilities honest, and the `hard` fit is exactly the
     sharpening that made v0's HelpSteer2 calibration worse."

Fitting temperature against the soft target you trained on is not the same as
fitting it against the argmax. We follow the `train` fit, and our
`fit_temperature` documents the choice.

4.5 getainode/jebadiah's own negative results
---------------------------------------------
Their experiment log is the most valuable single document in this field. Things
that did NOT work:

  - A 14,714-question synthetic pool authored by their own teachers made the 9B
    *worse*.
  - A fifth of synthetic choice questions helped a little.
  - Re-targeting synthetic data with a better teacher did not move the headline.
  - Public human yes/no data did not lift their human yes/no eval sets.
  - Two epochs were worse than one.

What DID work:

  - Adding human-rated score data and an ordinal target: HelpSteer2 Decision
    Score went from -21.4 to +11.9 and calibration error from 0.39 to 0.045.
    "The model became honest about not knowing helpfulness rather than better at
    judging it."
  - Switching the base checkpoint from Base to the chat checkpoint, with
    thinking off: worth 2.15 headline points on the 4B, more than any data change
    they made.

Read that last one twice. On the 4B, the single biggest win was the starting
checkpoint, not the data. That is why our default is the instruction-tuned
`LiquidAI/LFM2.5-350M`, not `-350M-Base`.

4.6 logan-markewich/jeff (400M, MIT)
------------------------------------
A different architecture entirely: GLiFormer, a bidirectional encoder, with a
temperature of 3.2 (i.e. substantially overconfident out of the box, corrected by
a large softening factor).

Its JevBench v1.2.2 score of 66.9 ranked #9, but on Intelligence it was #14 of 18.
Its own card: "Cheaper to self-host, but less accurate than jev on reasoning-heavy
tasks."

That is the honest shape of the small-model tradeoff, stated plainly by someone
who built one.

4.7 What converged and what did not
-----------------------------------
CONVERGED (independent implementations agree):
  - A small trained head, not constrained decoding.
  - A pretrained causal backbone, fine-tuned, not trained from scratch.
  - LoRA rather than full fine-tuning.
  - A fitted temperature, shipped with the model.
  - Per-question-type temperatures rather than one global value.
  - Reporting Brier/KL alongside ECE.
  - Explicitly labelling probability as a routing signal, not a truth claim.

DIVERGED (no consensus):
  - Slot logits (Jev-Omni) vs pointer scoring (Kev) vs label-token logits
    (Jebadiah). All three work. Slot logits are simplest and cheapest; we use them.
  - Causal decoder vs bidirectional encoder (jeff, LFM2.5-Encoder). Encoders are
    faster; decoders carry more pretrained knowledge. We use a decoder.
  - Whether questions should be isolated. Kev runs one row per question on hybrid
    DeltaNet bases because those layers ignore attention masks. We run one
    forward pass per question too, and say so.
  - Whether to use soft targets. Jebadiah says yes for rubrics; a benchmark says
    it cut KL by a third; Jebadiah also says a big synthetic soft-label pool made
    things worse. Both can be true: soft targets help, bad soft targets hurt.
